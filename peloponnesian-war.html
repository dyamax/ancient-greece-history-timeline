THE PELOPONNESIAN WAR

The Peloponnesian War (431–404 BC) was a protracted conflict between Athens and Sparta, alongside their respective allies, aiming for supremacy in the Greek world. The war began with the growth of Athenian power and Spartan apprehension, escalating into armed conflict. The Ten Years War phase, named after Spartan King Archidamus II, witnessed repeated Spartan invasions of Attica countered by Athenian naval raids, leading to a stalemate. The subsequent Peace of Nicias was marred by intermittent skirmishes and proxy battles, notably the Athenian-led Sicilian Expedition, which ended disastrously. The intervention of Persia in support of Sparta marked the Decelean War phase, resulting in Athens' defeat and the establishment of Spartan hegemony. The war's aftermath saw Sparta imposing oligarchies on former Athenian allies, reshaping the political landscape. Economically, Greece suffered widespread devastation, with Athens bearing the brunt. Socially, the conflict intensified existing rivalries between democratic Athens and oligarchic Sparta, contributing to a pervasive atmosphere of war. The Peloponnesian War represented a shift in Greek warfare, from ritualized conflicts to total war, with far-reaching consequences, marking the end of Greece's classical era and the dawn of a new geopolitical order.
